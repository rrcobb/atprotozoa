Jorge Luis Borges

1899–1986 · a reference page, and a note on librarians

Borges was an Argentine writer of short fictions, poems and essays, and for about thirty years a working librarian. He wrote about infinite libraries, forged bibliographies and encyclopedias that leak into the world, and he spent his days in actual reading rooms with actual card catalogues. This page is a list of what he wrote, a short life, some pointers on how he was read, and a commentary on why his job matters to the writing.

Works

Selective. Titles are the Spanish originals; the standard English collections are Labyrinths (1962), Collected Fictions (Andrew Hurley, 1998) and the Penguin Selected Poems (Alexander Coleman, ed., 1999). Many of the early essay books were pulled from his own bibliography and reappear only in scholarly editions.

Life

    He was blind by his mid-fifties, a condition he shared with his father and grandmother, and so stopped reading and writing by hand. After that he composed in his head, dictated to his mother, then to friends and secretaries, and favoured forms short enough to hold in memory: the sonnet, the milonga, the two-page story.

    Reception

    Before the 1960s he was a writer for writers in Buenos Aires, published in Sur and in small editions. The Formentor Prize in 1961 and the English translations of Ficciones and Labyrinths in 1962 turned him into a world author almost at once. After that:

    • Philosophy and theory. Michel Foucault opens The Order of Things with a laugh at a Chinese encyclopedia from Borges's essay on John Wilkins. Hypertext theorists in the 1990s took "The Garden of Forking Paths" as a prophecy of the link, and "Pierre Menard" has been argued over by translation and authorship scholars ever since.
    • Other writers. The Latin American Boom (Cortázar, García Márquez, Bolaño), John Barth's essay "The Literature of Exhaustion", Italo Calvino, and Umberto Eco, whose The Name of the Rose has a blind librarian called Jorge of Burgos and a labyrinthine library, and who admitted the debt.
    • Politics. His detractors in Argentina saw an aristocratic, anti-Peronist conservative who looked at the world through books. He welcomed the 1976 coup at first and took a medal from Pinochet in 1976, which is widely said to have cost him the Nobel; he then publicly criticised the juntas, the dirty war in particular, in the early 1980s. Opinions on that are still split.
    • Biography and criticism. Emir Rodríguez Monegal, Jorge Luis Borges: A Literary Biography (1978); Edwin Williamson, Borges: A Life (2004); Adolfo Bioy Casares, Borges (a posthumous diary of their friendship, 2006); Alberto Manguel, With Borges (a memoir of reading aloud to him as a teenager).

    The librarian as a knowledge node

    In 1937 he took a job as an assistant in a branch library on Buenos Aires's outskirts. By his own account (in the "Autobiographical Essay") his colleagues told him to slow down because he catalogued too quickly; the pace of the job was set by the people who'd be there next year. He wrote most of his famous fiction in those years, and later described those years as unhappy. In 1955 he was named director of the National Library, a post once held by another near-blind scholar, Paul Groussac. He wrote of that appointment that God had, "with magnificent irony", given him books and night together.

    "Yo, que me figuraba el paraíso / bajo la especie de una biblioteca."
    — "Poema de los dones" (1955)

    The fictions treat the same job as metaphysics. A librarian is a node in a network of texts: not the author of knowledge but the place where several authors' claims are shelved next to each other, where one thing can point to another, and where the order is itself an argument. Look at what his stories do with that:

    • "The Library of Babel": every possible book exists, so nearly every book is noise, and the librarians' whole life is a search problem. Whoever finds the catalogue of catalogues becomes a kind of god; nobody finds it.
    • "Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius": a single encyclopedia entry, an imaginary country, and then the world quietly reorganises itself around a thing that was only ever an entry. The index precedes the territory.
    • "Funes the Memorious": a man who forgets nothing and so cannot think. To organise is to throw most things away.
    • "On Exactitude in Science": a map as large as the empire. A catalogue as big as the collection is no catalogue.
    • "Pierre Menard", "Examination of the Work of Herbert Quain": reviews and annotations of books that do not exist. The citation is the unit of literature and it can be forged.

    The through-line is that a library is a set of choices about what sits beside what, and that choice is the real knowledge. He read and wrote at the junction between works, and treated influence as running in both directions ("Kafka and His Precursors": each writer creates his own precursors by changing how we read the earlier ones).
