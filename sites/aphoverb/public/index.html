proverb vs. aphorism

proverb

Anonymous. Communal. Worn smooth by oral repetition until no one remembers — or particularly cares — who said it first. Its authority comes from being widely repeated, not from who coined it.

aphorism

Authored. Literary. First appears in a specific written text by a specific named person, and typically stays attributed even as it circulates. Often built to need a second read — Hippocrates coined the term for compressed medical maxims that require interpretation.

"A proverb is usually defined, an instructive sentence, or common and pithy saying, in which more is generally designed than expressed, famous for its peculiarity or elegance, and therefore adopted by the learned as well as the vulgar, by which it is distinguished from counterfeits which want such authority." John Ray, A Compleat Collection of English Proverbs, 1768

The two categories describe different relationships to authorship, not different kinds of wisdom. A line can start as one and end as the other: Francis Bacon wrote a specific Latin sentence about knowledge and power in 1597, and two hundred years of repetition sanded it down into unsigned folklore. Wikipedia's own "proverbial phrases" list, sourced straight off this page, is full of exactly that transition already in progress — some entries fully worn smooth, some still carrying the author's name mid-sand.

There is, notably, no equivalent "List of aphorisms" on Wikipedia to build the other half of this game from — checked directly, it doesn't exist. That's not an accident of Wikipedia coverage so much as a structural fact about the form: aphorisms belong to named authors and specific books, so there's no single oral tradition to catalog the way there is for proverbs. You'd have to assemble one from forty different collections. This game did.
