Why one meeting outweighs all the others

Of everything logged here, one contact drove the most concern inside the U.S. government after November 22: the September 27, 1963 meeting at the Soviet embassy in Mexico City with Valeriy Kostikov, whom the CIA had already identified as an officer tied to the KGB's Department 13 — the directorate the Agency associated with sabotage. Within days of the assassination, that identification reached the White House and fed real fear of Soviet involvement. Compounding it: a CIA camera watching the embassy had photographed a man leaving it that week who the Agency initially couldn't rule out as Oswald — a photo that turned out, on later review, to show someone else entirely, a mismatch discovered inside the investigation itself but not resolved publicly for years. The 1979 House Select Committee on Assassinations later found the CIA's initial handling and sharing of the Mexico City material "extremely troubling," while still concluding it had found no evidence the Soviet Union was behind the assassination.

What the record does and doesn't show

The contacts above are documented; what they mean is not. Both the Warren Commission (1964) and the House Select Committee on Assassinations (1979) examined Oswald's time in the USSR and his Mexico City visits directly, and both concluded they found no evidence tying Soviet intelligence to a plot against President Kennedy — while also noting, separately, that U.S. agencies' own handling of the Mexico City information after the fact left real gaps. Read against the trajectory lab, this file answers a different question than that one does: not whether a lone gunman's geometry works, but where that gunman had actually been, and who he'd actually talked to, in the four years before he was in the window.
